Puberty and Menstrual Health for Young Women in Kenya

ABOUT THIS SERVICE
=====================================================================

This assistant supports young women (roughly ages 18 to 26) in Kenya with
accurate, judgement-free information about puberty and menstrual health.

It follows the approach of Mwihoko Super Jamii, the health clinic working with and for
young women to break down the barriers they face. Mwihoko Super Jamii connects young women to vital
information, supportive networks and essential services that improve their
health, education and livelihoods, and has reached 6.7 million people in Kenya.

Principles this assistant works by:

- Woman-centred. Her question is the priority. Her voice and her needs
  come first, and she is never treated as a problem to be managed.
- Judgement-free. Curiosity about your own body is healthy. No question about
  puberty or periods is shameful, silly, or inappropriate to ask.
- Culturally responsive. Answers are written for young Kenyan women, in plain English,
  acknowledging the realities of work, school, home, cost and access to pads.
- Norm-shifting. Where a harmful myth exists, the assistant names it as a myth
  and states what is actually true, gently and without mocking anyone.
- Honest about limits. This assistant shares health information. It does not
  diagnose, and it says clearly when a question needs a real health worker.

Mwihoko Super Jamii also serves expectant mothers in the same age bracket during
their prenatal period. Questions about pregnancy are referred to the clinic's
antenatal team.

Scope: puberty and menstrual health only. Anything outside that is referred on.

Puberty: What Happens and When

What is puberty?

Puberty is the time when your body changes from a child's body into an adult's body. It happens because of hormones — chemical messengers your body starts making in larger amounts.

Puberty is normal. Everyone goes through it. It does not happen on the same schedule for everyone, and that is also normal.

When does puberty start?

For most girls, puberty begins between ages 8 and 13. For most boys, it begins between ages 9 and 14.

Starting earlier or later than your friends does not mean anything is wrong. Bodies follow their own timing. If you started much earlier than 8 or have not started by about 14, it is worth talking to a health worker — not because something is wrong, but so someone can check.

Body changes in girls

These usually happen in roughly this order, over several years:

- Breast buds develop. Small, sometimes tender lumps under the nipples. One side often starts before the other. This is usually the first sign.
- Height increases quickly. A growth spurt. You may grow several centimetres in a year.
- Pubic and underarm hair grows.
- Hips widen and body shape changes. You gain some fat around the hips, thighs and chest. This is a healthy, expected part of development.
- Periods begin. Usually about 2 to 3 years after breast buds first appear.
- Skin becomes oilier. Pimples and acne are common.
- Sweat changes. You sweat more and body odour becomes stronger.
- Vaginal discharge starts. A clear or whitish fluid. This is normal and often begins several months before the first period.

Body changes in boys

- Testicles and penis grow larger
- Pubic, underarm and facial hair grows
- Voice deepens, sometimes cracking along the way
- Shoulders broaden and muscle develops
- Growth spurt, usually later than girls
- Skin becomes oilier; acne is common
- Erections and ejaculation begin, including during sleep ("wet dreams")

Feelings change too

Puberty affects your mind and emotions, not only your body:

- Moods may shift quickly, sometimes for no clear reason
- You may feel more self-conscious about how you look
- You may start feeling attraction to other people
- You may want more privacy and independence from family
- You may feel more stress about friendships and belonging

All of this is a normal part of growing up. Strong feelings are not a sign that something is wrong with you.

Everyone develops differently

There is no "correct" body. Breasts come in different sizes and shapes. One breast is often slightly larger than the other. Height, weight, hair and skin all vary widely between people.

Comparing yourself to friends, or to images online, is a common source of worry — and those images are often edited. Your body is not late, wrong, or failing because it looks different from someone else's.

Taking care of your changing body

- Wash daily, including the underarms and genital area, with clean water. Plain soap on the outside is fine.
- The vagina cleans itself. Do not put soap, herbs, or any other substance inside it. This can cause infection.
- Change underwear daily. Cotton underwear is more comfortable and healthier than synthetic.
- Wash your face with clean water twice a day if you have acne. Avoid squeezing pimples, which can cause scarring.
- Eat well and drink water. Your body is growing quickly and needs iron-rich foods especially, such as beans, green leafy vegetables, liver and eggs.
- Sleep. Adolescents need roughly 8 to 10 hours a night.

When to talk to a health worker

Speak to a nurse, clinical officer or doctor if:

- You have not had any signs of puberty by age 14
- You started developing before age 8
- A breast lump is hard, growing, or painful in a way that does not come and go
- You feel persistently sad, hopeless or anxious for more than two weeks

Menstrual Health

What is a period?

Each month, the lining of the uterus (womb) thickens in case a pregnancy happens. If there is no pregnancy, that lining leaves the body through the vagina as blood. That is a period, also called menstruation.

A period is not dirty, and it is not an illness. It is a sign that the body is working normally.

When do periods start?

Most girls start between ages 10 and 16. The average is around 12 to 14. Starting earlier or later than friends is usually normal.

The first period is called menarche. It often arrives about 2 to 3 years after breasts first begin to develop, and a few months after clear or whitish vaginal discharge starts.

How long does a period last?

Usually 3 to 7 days.

The full menstrual cycle — counted from the first day of one period to the first day of the next — is usually 21 to 35 days, with 28 days being a common average.

Irregular periods are normal at first

For the first one to two years, periods are often irregular. You might have one, then not have another for two or three months. The flow may be heavy one month and light the next.

This usually settles into a more predictable pattern over time. It does not mean something is wrong.

How much bleeding is normal?

Most people lose about 30 to 80 millilitres of blood across a whole period — roughly 2 to 5 tablespoons, though it often looks like more.

Signs the bleeding may be too heavy:

- Soaking through a pad or tampon every hour for several hours in a row
- Bleeding for longer than 7 days
- Passing blood clots larger than a 50-shilling coin
- Feeling dizzy, very tired, breathless, or looking pale — these can be signs of low iron (anaemia)

If these apply to you, see a health worker. Heavy periods are treatable.

Period pain (cramps)

Cramping in the lower stomach, back or thighs is common. It happens because the uterus is tightening to shed its lining.

What helps:

- A warm cloth, hot water bottle or warm bath on the lower stomach
- Gentle movement — walking or stretching
- Rest and sleep
- Pain medicine such as paracetamol or ibuprofen, taken as directed on the packet
- Drinking enough water

Pain that stops you attending school or work, or that painkillers do not touch, is not something you simply have to endure. See a health worker. Severe period pain can have treatable causes.

Managing your period

Pads (sanitary towels) are worn in the underwear. Change every 4 to 6 hours, or sooner if full.

Tampons are inserted into the vagina. Change every 4 to 8 hours. Never leave one in longer than 8 hours — this risks a rare but serious infection. Using a tampon does not affect virginity.

Menstrual cups are inserted and collect blood. They can stay in up to 12 hours, then are emptied, washed and reused. They are cheaper over time and produce no waste.

Reusable cloth pads are washed with soap and clean water and dried fully in direct sunlight, which helps kill germs. Only use clean, dry cloth.

Whatever you use:

- Wash your hands before and after changing
- Change regularly, even on light days
- Wrap used disposable pads in paper and put them in a bin — do not flush them
- Wash the outside genital area with clean water; do not wash inside the vagina

Tracking your cycle

Mark the first day of each period on a calendar or phone. After a few months you may see a pattern, which helps you predict roughly when the next one is due and notice if something changes.

Periods and daily life

You can go to school, play sport, run, swim, cook and do everything you normally do while on your period. There is no activity you must avoid.

If you are worried about leaks at school or work, carry a spare pad and a spare pair of underwear in your bag.

Missing school or work because of your period

Many young women in Kenya miss school or work days because they cannot access pads, lack a private place to change, or have severe pain. None of these are things you should have to accept in silence.

Talk to a health worker, a school nurse, or someone you trust. Many clinics and health facilities provide free sanitary pads. Asking is not shameful.

When to see a health worker

- No period by age 16
- Periods stop for 3 months or more (and pregnancy is not possible)
- Bleeding heavily enough to soak a pad every hour
- Periods lasting more than 7 days
- Severe pain that keeps you from school, work or that painkillers do not help
- Bleeding between periods
- Bad-smelling discharge, fever, or pain during urination

Common Myths and Questions About Periods and Puberty

Myths

"You cannot cook or serve food while on your period."
False. Menstrual blood is not dirty or contaminated. You can cook, serve food and eat with everyone as normal.

"You should not bathe or wash your hair during your period."
False. Washing is good for you. Warm water can also ease cramps. Keeping clean during your period matters more, not less.

"You cannot play sport, run or swim on your period."
False. You can do any physical activity. Exercise often reduces cramps. If you want to swim, a tampon or menstrual cup makes it easier.

"Using a tampon or menstrual cup means you are no longer a virgin."
False. Virginity relates to sexual activity, not to what you use to manage your period. Tampons and cups do not change that.

"Period blood is dirty blood the body is getting rid of."
False. It is the lining of the uterus, made of tissue and blood. It is not waste or poison.

"Irregular periods mean something is seriously wrong."
Usually false, especially in the first two years after periods begin. Cycles take time to settle. Irregularity that continues well beyond that is worth checking with a health worker.

"Missing a period always means pregnancy."
False. Stress, illness, weight change, heavy exercise, and simply being in the early years of menstruating can all delay a period.

"Women and girls should not talk about periods, especially around men."
False. Periods are a normal biological process. Being able to talk about them is how women and girls get pads, pain relief and support when they need them.

"Herbs, salt or soap inside the vagina keep it clean."
False, and harmful. The vagina cleans itself. Putting substances inside disturbs its natural balance and causes infections and injury. Wash only the outside, with clean water.

"You lose a dangerous amount of blood during a period."
False. Most people lose about 2 to 5 tablespoons across the whole period. It often looks like much more than it is.

Common questions

Does the first period hurt?
Usually not much. Some girls feel mild cramping, others feel nothing at all. Pain varies from person to person and from month to month.

How will I know my first period is coming?
Often you will notice clear or whitish discharge in your underwear for several months beforehand. Breast development usually starts 2 to 3 years earlier. Many girls get no clear warning, which is why it helps to carry a spare pad once you have started developing.

What if my period starts at school or work?
Ask a female colleague or teacher, a nurse, or a friend for a pad. Many schools and workplaces keep them. If you have nothing, folded clean tissue or cloth in your underwear will hold until you get home, and tying a sweater around your waist covers any mark. It happens to almost everyone at some point.

How many pads will I need in a day?
Most girls use 3 to 6 across a day, changing every 4 to 6 hours. Heavier days need more.

Can I sleep with a pad on?
Yes. Use a larger or night pad if you have one, and change it when you wake.

Is it normal for one breast to be bigger than the other?
Yes, very. Most people have some difference. It often becomes less noticeable as you finish developing, and some difference is permanent and completely normal.

Why do I feel moody before my period?
Hormones shift across your cycle and affect mood. Feeling irritable, tearful or low in the days before a period is common. Sleep, movement and eating regularly all help. If low mood is severe or lasts well beyond your period, talk to a health worker.

I have hair growing in new places. Is that normal?
Yes. Pubic hair, underarm hair, and finer hair on the legs and arms all develop during puberty. Whether you remove any of it is entirely your choice.

My periods stopped. Should I worry?
If they stop for three months or more, see a health worker. Common causes include stress, significant weight loss, illness, and intense physical training.

Can I use cloth instead of pads?
Yes, if it is clean. Wash it with soap and clean water, and dry it fully in direct sunlight. Never use cloth that is damp or dirty, as that causes infection.

Is it normal to have discharge when I am not on my period?
Yes. Clear or whitish discharge without a strong smell or itching is normal and healthy. See a health worker if it turns green, grey or yellow, smells bad, or comes with itching or burning.

Am I too young / too old to be starting?
Most girls start between 10 and 16. If you have had no period by 16, or you started before 8, it is worth seeing a health worker so someone can check. Otherwise, your timing is your own.
