# Sexual Health and Safety

## Consent

Sex should only happen when both people freely agree. You can say no at any time — before or during — to anyone, including a boyfriend or a husband. Being pressured, threatened, tricked, or too drunk to decide is not consent.

## Sexually transmitted infections (STIs)

STIs are common, and many have no signs at all. Possible signs include:

- Unusual discharge from the vagina, or a bad smell
- Sores, blisters or warts around the genitals
- Pain or burning when passing urine
- Pain in the lower belly, or pain during sex
- Bleeding between periods or after sex

Most STIs are easy to treat, and treatment is available at health facilities. If you are treated, your partner needs treatment too, or the infection can come back. Untreated STIs can cause pain, problems getting pregnant later, and harm in pregnancy.

Condoms, used every time, are the best protection against STIs.

## HIV

- **Testing** is free, quick and confidential at health facilities. Knowing your status is the first step to staying healthy.
- **Treatment** lets people living with HIV live long, healthy lives. When the virus is kept low with treatment, it is not passed on through sex.
- **PrEP** is a daily pill that protects people who do not have HIV but are at higher risk. It is free at public health facilities.
- **PEP** is medicine taken after a possible exposure to HIV, such as unprotected sex or sexual violence. It must start as soon as possible, and within 3 days (72 hours). It is taken for 28 days.

## Cervical cancer

Cervical cancer can be prevented. The HPV vaccine is given to girls in Kenya to protect them before they become sexually active. Screening finds early changes that can be treated before they become cancer. Kenya recommends screening for women from age 25 — earlier and more often for women living with HIV. It is quick, and free at many public facilities.

## After sexual violence

If someone has forced or pressured you into sex, it is not your fault. You do not have to face it alone.

**Go to a health facility as soon as you can — ideally within 3 days (72 hours).** There, a health worker can:

- Give you PEP to prevent HIV
- Give you emergency contraception to prevent pregnancy
- Test and treat you for other infections
- Treat any injuries, and offer counselling
- Fill in the medical form that can support a police report, free of charge, if you decide to report

If you can, keep the clothes you were wearing and try not to bathe before you go — but go even if you already have. Care is still worth getting after 72 hours. Some hospitals have special gender-based violence recovery centres.

Call the **national GBV helpline on 1195** — free, confidential and open 24 hours. If you are in danger right now, call **999 or 112**.

## Violence from a partner

Being hit, threatened, controlled, kept from money or friends, or forced into sex by a partner is abuse. It often gets worse over time, and it can happen during pregnancy. It is not your fault. The 1195 helpline can help you think through your options and stay safe.
