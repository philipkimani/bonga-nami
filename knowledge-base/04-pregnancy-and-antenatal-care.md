# Pregnancy, Antenatal Care and After the Birth

## Could I be pregnant?

Common early signs are a missed period, feeling sick or vomiting (often in the morning, but it can be any time), sore or fuller breasts, needing to pass urine more often, and feeling very tired. Some women have light spotting around the time their period was due.

A pregnancy test from a chemist or a health facility can confirm it. Tests are most reliable from the first day of a missed period. A health facility can test for free and talk you through what comes next.

## What is antenatal care?

Antenatal care — also called prenatal care — is the check-ups you have while you are pregnant. They protect your health and your baby's health, and they catch problems early, when they are easiest to treat.

Start as early as you can, ideally before 12 weeks of pregnancy. The World Health Organization recommends at least eight antenatal contacts during a pregnancy. Kenya follows this guidance.

Maternity care, including antenatal visits and giving birth, is free at public health facilities in Kenya. Ask the clinic what applies to you.

## What happens at an antenatal visit?

At your first visits, the health worker will usually:

- Check your blood pressure and weight
- Test your blood for anaemia (low blood), your blood group, HIV, syphilis and hepatitis B
- Test your urine
- Work out how far along you are, sometimes with an ultrasound scan
- Give you iron and folic acid tablets
- Give you the tetanus vaccine if you need it
- Give you a Mother and Child Health booklet — bring it to every visit

At later visits they check how the baby is growing, listen to the baby's heartbeat, and keep checking your blood pressure. These visits are also the time to ask anything at all.

## Iron and folic acid

Iron helps prevent anaemia, which is common in pregnancy and makes you tired and weak. Folic acid helps the baby's brain and spine form properly. Take the iron and folic acid tablets the clinic gives you every day for the whole pregnancy. Folic acid helps most in the very early weeks, so starting before pregnancy is even better.

## What should I eat when I am pregnant?

You do not need special or expensive food. You need a variety of ordinary food, and a little more of it.

- **Eat a little more each day.** An extra small meal or snack is enough.
- **Beans, ndengu, lentils, eggs, milk, fish such as omena, and meat** give protein and iron.
- **Dark green vegetables** such as sukuma wiki, managu and terere give iron and folic acid.
- **Fruit** of any kind, every day if you can.
- **Ugali, githeri, rice, potatoes and bread** give energy. Whole grains are best.
- **Use iodised salt** — most salt sold in Kenya is iodised.
- **Drink plenty of clean, safe water.** Boil or treat it if you are not sure.

Some things are best avoided while pregnant:

- **Alcohol** — no amount is known to be safe in pregnancy.
- **Smoking, miraa and other drugs.**
- **Raw or undercooked meat and eggs**, and milk that has not been boiled.
- **Too much tea or coffee** — a cup or two a day is fine.

Some women crave soil, clay or soft stones. This can be a sign of anaemia. Do not eat them — tell the health worker instead.

If you feel sick, small meals more often, and dry food such as bread in the morning, can help. If you cannot keep any food or water down, see a health worker.

## Common discomforts

Feeling sick in early pregnancy, heartburn, constipation, back pain, leg cramps and tiredness are all common. They usually ease with time. Eating smaller meals, drinking water, eating fruit and vegetables, gentle walking and resting help. Mention any discomfort at your antenatal visit — it is what the visits are for.

## Staying well in pregnancy

- **Keep active.** Walking and your normal daily work are good for you unless a health worker says otherwise.
- **Sleep under a treated mosquito net** every night. Malaria is more dangerous in pregnancy. In areas with malaria, the clinic may also give you medicine to prevent it.
- **Know your HIV status.** If you are living with HIV, treatment during pregnancy and breastfeeding can almost always stop the virus passing to your baby.
- **Sex during pregnancy** is usually safe unless a health worker tells you otherwise.

## Danger signs in pregnancy — go to a health facility now

Do not wait for your next visit if you have any of these:

- Bleeding from the vagina
- Severe pain in the belly
- A severe headache, blurred vision, or fits (convulsions)
- Swelling of the face or hands
- Fever
- Water leaking from the vagina before labour
- The baby moving less than usual, or not moving
- Difficulty breathing, or feeling very weak

## Getting ready for the birth

Plan to give birth at a health facility, with a skilled health worker. Most problems at birth can be handled safely when help is close.

- Know which facility you will go to, and how you will get there day or night.
- Save a little money for transport and needs.
- Choose someone to go with you.
- Pack a bag: your Mother and Child Health booklet, clothes for you and the baby, sanitary pads and a wrap.

Signs that labour has started: regular pains that get stronger and closer together, a "show" of mucus with a little blood, or your waters breaking. Go to the facility.

## After the birth

You and your baby should be checked within the first day, again within the first week, and at six weeks. These checks are for you as well as the baby.

- **Breastfeeding.** Start within the first hour. Give only breast milk — no water, no other food — for the first six months.
- **Baby's vaccinations** start at birth. Keep the baby's clinic card and follow the schedule.
- **Rest and eat well.** Keep taking any tablets the clinic gives you.

Go to a health facility now if **the mother** has heavy bleeding, fever, a bad-smelling discharge, a severe headache, or fits — or if **the baby** is not feeding, has a fever or feels very cold, breathes fast or with difficulty, has fits, or has yellow skin in the first day.

Feeling low or tearful in the first days after birth is common. If sadness, worry or hopelessness lasts more than two weeks, or you have thoughts of harming yourself or the baby, tell a health worker — help works.

You can get pregnant again before your periods come back. Talk to the clinic about family planning after the birth. Waiting at least two years before the next pregnancy is healthiest for you and your next baby.
